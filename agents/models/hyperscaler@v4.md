# Thesis: hyperscaler (hyperscaler@v4)

## Thesis

The hyperscalers are the buyers. Their capex is the AI buildout, and the server and network category of PP&E is where the GPUs land. Capex allocation is the measure that matters most here; project-level investment (named campuses, power deals, cloud commitments to neoclouds, third-party-financed ventures) and plans versus reality (capex guidance against the actual) matter next. Revenue exposure matters least: these companies sell ads, software and cloud, and no reported segment is the AI one. The signal to watch is the year-on-year growth in the gross value of the server and network category, read against the company's own capex guidance and against purchase commitments and leases not yet commenced, which run a year or more ahead.

The scale, from the filings read for this version: the four largest spent $360 billion of cash capex in their 2025 fiscal years (Amazon $131.8 billion, Alphabet $91.4 billion, Meta $69.7 billion, Microsoft $64.6 billion for the year to June 2025), and Microsoft's year to June 2026 was $115.9 billion. Dell'Oro puts the top four US cloud providers at over $340 billion of data-center capex in 2025, up 76 percent, and forecasts worldwide data-center capex above $1 trillion in 2026. Guidance for 2026 is Amazon about $200 billion, Alphabet $175 to $185 billion, Meta $115 to $135 billion.

## Companies

Microsoft (MSFT), Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN), Meta (META) and Oracle (ORCL). The first four are the model: huge capex, a server category in the PP&E note, guidance "to support AI". Oracle sits at the edge: it is a hyperscaler by spend but its buildout is contracted to one customer class, its remaining performance obligations are the forward number, and customers now prepay or supply the GPUs; a reader should treat it as a buyer with a seller's backlog. Apple and Tesla also buy compute but do not report like this; they sit in `other`.

## Where the AI money shows up

1. **Capex** (`values.capex`, cash paid for property and equipment) is the main line, but it understates the buildout: finance leases (`facts.finance_lease_liability`: Microsoft $66.6 billion, Amazon $12.3 billion, Oracle $7.7 billion at the latest year-end), leases not yet commenced (Microsoft $329.1 billion, Oracle $260 billion, Meta $103.8 billion) and third-party-funded datacenters (Meta's Louisiana venture, 20 percent owned, $27 billion to build) sit outside it. Meta's own capex figure includes finance lease principal; the ledger's `values.capex` does not.
2. **PP&E by category** (`facts.ppe_category`): the server category (Microsoft "Servers, network equipment, and software"; Meta "Servers and network assets"; Alphabet "Technical infrastructure", called "Information technology assets" before the FY2024 10-K, the same category renamed; Amazon "Servers and networking equipment"; Oracle "Computer, network, machinery and equipment") is where GPUs land. Its year-on-year growth in gross value is the hard number closest to compute capex: Microsoft +$83.0 billion in FY2026, Alphabet +$61.8 billion, Amazon +$59.3 billion, Meta +$29.6 billion in 2025, Oracle +$29.3 billion in FY2026. Buildings, land and construction in progress are the shell, not AI as such; construction in progress is large (Amazon $71.7 billion, Meta $50.5 billion, Oracle $40.0 billion) and mixed.
3. **What the company says** (`ai_spend`): these companies give total-capex guidance "to support AI" (`scope: total`: Meta $115 to $135 billion for 2026, Alphabet $175 to $185 billion, Amazon about $200 billion) and, on calls, sometimes a split (Alphabet: about 60 percent servers, 40 percent data centers and networking, Q4 2025 call). An explicit AI share from the company (`scope: ai`, `explicit_share`) beats any estimate; none of the five gave one in the filings read.
4. **Purchase commitments** (`facts.purchase_commitments`) are forward: contracted GPUs and cloud capacity (Meta $131.0 billion, mostly third-party cloud capacity; Alphabet $149.1 billion). A jump in commitments precedes capex by a year. Remaining performance obligations (Microsoft $678 billion, Oracle $638 billion, Amazon $244 billion, Alphabet $243 billion) are demand, not spend; they belong in `basis`.

## How to attribute

Capex, in order of hardness:

- `stated_share`: the company states the AI (or GPU/server) share of capex → `ai_amount = share × capex`. Alphabet's "approximately 60% ... in servers" is a server share, not an AI share; use it only as `ppe_category` support, confidence ≤ 0.6, and name the call.
- `stated_amount`: the company states an AI dollar amount for the year (`scope: ai`, `explicit_amount`). None of the five did in the filings read for this version.
- `ppe_category`: growth in the server/network category: `ai_amount = category gross(t) − category gross(t−1)`, when both years are in `facts`. Name the category. This counts all server growth, not only AI; say so in `basis` and set confidence ≤ 0.6.
- `qualitative`: the company says "primarily AI" (Amazon's "primarily reflects investments in artificial intelligence", Meta's "to support our AI efforts and core business") but gives nothing to compute from: no amount, confidence ≤ 0.3.
- `none`: nothing links spend to AI.

Opex: AI shows up as depreciation of the fleet (Alphabet $21.1 billion in 2025, up 38 percent) and as R&D headcount; no hyperscaler quantifies the AI part. Expect `qualitative` or `none` for opex.

Revenue, in order of hardness:

- `stated_amount`: the company states an AI revenue figure for the year. Microsoft's $37 billion AI run rate (April 29 2026) is a run-rate, not a fiscal-year figure; confidence ≤ 0.5 and say so in `basis`.
- `stated_share`: the company states AI's share of revenue, or of a segment's growth. A growth contribution ("AI contributed N points to Azure growth") is not a share of revenue; record it in `basis`, not as a share. Confidence ≤ 0.8 for a true share.
- `segment`: not available. Microsoft Intelligent Cloud is not AI; Google Cloud ($58.7 billion in 2025) is not AI; AWS ($128.7 billion) is not AI; Oracle's cloud infrastructure line is not AI. Never sum a cloud segment as the AI line.
- `qualitative`: cloud growth or backlog attributed to AI in words only, confidence ≤ 0.3.
- `none`: the common answer, and the honest one for Meta and Amazon.
- `whole_business`: never.

## Projects

A project is a named, located, sized capital commitment: a data-center campus with a site and a megawatt or gigawatt figure (Meta's Hyperion and Prometheus, Oracle's Abilene site, Microsoft's Fairwater), a power deal with a named plant or counterparty (Microsoft's Crane Clean Energy Center PPA), a joint venture or third-party financing for a named site (Meta's Louisiana venture: 20 percent owned, $27 billion estimated development cost, $45.95 billion maximum exposure), or a multi-year capacity commitment to a neocloud with an amount. Words that name one in releases and MD&A: "campus", "site", "broke ground", "gigawatt", "megawatts", "joint venture", "venture", "lease agreement", "commitment", "power purchase agreement", "maximum exposure to loss". Not a project: a model or product launch (Copilot, Gemini), a new chip generation, a hire, total-capex guidance with no site, a partnership with no amount.

## Traps

- A total-capex figure "to support our AI efforts" is the whole, never the AI part. Never copy it into `ai_amount`.
- Gross PP&E categories are cumulative balances; the flow is the difference between year-ends, and a disposal or reclassification can make it negative (then `none`).
- Meta's reported capex includes principal payments on finance leases ($72.22 billion for 2025 against $69.69 billion of purchases); `values.capex` is the purchases line. Say which one `basis` uses.
- Opex: AI shows up as depreciation of the fleet and as R&D headcount; no hyperscaler quantifies it. Expect `qualitative` or `none` for opex.
- A cloud segment (Intelligent Cloud, Google Cloud, AWS) is not AI revenue. An AI growth contribution in points is not a share of revenue. A run rate is not a year.
- Remaining performance obligations (Oracle $638 billion, Microsoft $678 billion) are contracted future revenue, not a project and not revenue in the year.
- Oracle's customer prepayments and customer-supplied GPUs ($75 billion) reduce Oracle's own capex; the GPUs the customer supplies are the customer's spend, not Oracle's.
- Leases not yet commenced are neither capex nor a liability yet; record the amount in `basis` as a forward signal, never in `ai_amount`.
- A capacity deal where the hyperscaler is the customer (Microsoft buying from CoreWeave, Meta's third-party cloud commitments) is a project for the hyperscaler and revenue for the seller; record who pays.
